## Draft text

### Research Question 3: Which components combine the highest waste with the lowest capability, and how does the ranking shift with scale, sequence length and precision?

The capability overlay ranks the components by their contribution to model quality, from the ablation and removal studies admitted in Phase 1 (Table 3): rank 1 is the component whose removal or reduction costs the most quality. The ranking is ordinal and is applied to every configuration alike, because the published evidence does not vary with the sampled configurations. The cross-entropy loss is the training objective rather than an architectural component and is not ranked. Each component's waste is summarized as the median of its diagnostic waste score over the eighteen configurations, with the range over configurations shown alongside. The two are combined in a quadrant view: waste is split at a score of zero, and capability at the median rank of the seven ranked components, rank 4, which is placed in the lower-capability half, so that ranks 1 to 3 count as high capability. Table 8 gives the ranking and its evidence, and Figure 7 places each component in its quadrant.

**Table 8.** Capability ranking, capability evidence and pooled waste score per component

|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | Attention core | Dropping 48 attention layers: Llama-2-70B 73.9 to 66.9; the whole attention sublayer is removed (He, Sun, et al., 2024) | +0.070 (+0.007 to +0.312) | High waste, high capability |
| 3 | Output projection | Output embedding 768 to 128: average 60.2 to 56.6, multilingual encoder (H. W. Chung et al., 2021) | -0.005 (-0.123 to +0.008) | Low waste, high capability |
| 4 | Input embedding lookup | Factorized to 128 dimensions: average 82.3 to 81.7, ALBERT-base (Lan et al., 2020) | +0.0004 (+0.00005 to +0.002) | High waste, low capability |
| 5 | Attention projections | Key-value heads in 8 groups: 47.2 to 47.1, T5-XXL (Ainslie et al., 2023); value and output projections fixed to identity: loss 1.178, against 1.194 when trained (He & Hofmann, 2024) | -0.050 (-0.096 to -0.013) | Low waste, low capability |
| 6 | Positional encoding | No positional encoding: perplexity 20.42 to 20.97 (247M) and 13.05 to 13.10 (1.3B) (Haviv et al., 2022) | +0.016 (+0.004 to +0.042) | High waste, low capability |

*Note.* Rank 1 is the largest contribution to model quality. The evidence comes from other models (Llama-2, T5, ALBERT, a multilingual encoder and others), metrics and kinds of intervention (removal, reduction, replacement), so only the order is used, not the size of the effects. Pooled C4 is the median over the eighteen configurations of each component's median score. The cross-entropy loss (pooled C4 +0.004) is not ranked.

**Figure 7.** Pooled diagnostic waste score against capability rank, with the quadrant boundaries (repo `data/figures/F8`). *Note.* Points show the median score over the eighteen configurations and whiskers its range; red points are positive scores. The vertical line separates ranks 1 to 3 (high capability) from ranks 4 to 7.

**Capability ordering.** The feedforward sublayer ranks first: removing feedforward layers cost more quality than removing any other sublayer in the admitted studies (He, Sun, et al., 2024). The attention core ranks second. Removing attention layers also costs several points, but that evidence removes the whole attention sublayer, so it does not separate the core from the projections; the core is ranked above the projections because reducing the number of key and value heads, which shrinks the projections, cost almost nothing. The output projection ranks third, since shrinking it cost 3.6 points in a multilingual encoder, and the input embedding lookup fourth, since factorizing it cost 0.6 points; no study removes the input embedding outright. Positional encoding ranks sixth, because language models trained without any positional encoding reached nearly the same perplexity (Haviv et al., 2022; Kazemnejad et al., 2023). That evidence removes positional information entirely, while Pythia applies rotary encoding to only a quarter of each head, so it bounds the cost from above. Layer normalization ranks last, with a qualification: replacing it with Dynamic Tanh left the average zero-shot score unchanged (Zhu et al., 2025, with RMSNorm rather than LayerNorm as the baseline), but removing it outright left no usable downstream performance (He & Hofmann, 2024). Its rank means replaceable, not dispensable.

**Priority components.** Three components combine positive waste with low capability: positional encoding (pooled score +0.016), layer normalization (+0.011) and the input embedding lookup (+0.0004). They are the priority targets the first proposition describes, and all three are memory-bound components for which H1 was supported under the first research question. Ordered by waste, positional encoding comes first, then layer normalization, then the lookup, and this order holds in every one of the eighteen configurations. The lookup's place rests on two thin margins: its waste score is the smallest of any memory-bound component, and its capability rank is the median rank, placed in the lower half by convention; were it placed in the upper half, the priority set would contain positional encoding and layer normalization only. Under this convention, the attention core is the only component with high waste and high capability. It has the largest pooled waste in the profile and the largest score in 15 of the 18 configurations, but because the model depends on it, it is a target only for substitutions that preserve what it computes. The feedforward sublayer and the output projection are high-capability components with negative pooled scores, and the attention projections are low in both waste and capability; none of the three offers an energy argument for intervention on this evidence.

**Shifts with the configuration moderators.** Figure 8 compares each component's waste rank between configurations that differ in one moderator only: model scale from 160M to 6.9B (six pairs), sequence length from 512 to 2,048 tokens (six pairs) and precision from FP32 to FP16 (nine pairs). Sequence length moved the ranking least: 8 of the 48 component and pair results changed, by at most two places, and positional encoding, layer normalization, the attention core and the feedforward sublayer never moved. Longer sequences raise the attention core's score (second research question), but it already ranks first wherever the feedforward sublayer does not. Scale moved the ranking most, changing 35 of 48 results: the loss fell three places in every pair (from second to fifth), the attention projections fell one or two places, the lookup fell one, and the output projection rose three places in the FP16 pairs. Precision changed 22 of 72 results, mostly by one place and concentrated at Pythia 6.9B. The largest shifts, seven places, belong to the feedforward sublayer at Pythia 6.9B FP32, which rises from last to first with scale and falls from first to last with precision; they track its positive score at those configurations, which did not repeat in every run (second research question), and should be read with that caveat rather than as a clean moderator effect. The priority components are the most stable part of the ranking. Positional encoding and layer normalization moved by at most one place under any moderator and never with sequence length, the lookup moved by at most one place, and the attention core kept its rank in 15 of 21 comparisons, moving one place only where the feedforward sublayer overtook it at Pythia 6.9B FP32.

**Figure 8.** Change in each component's waste rank under each configuration moderator (repo `data/figures/F9`). *Note.* Each column compares two configurations that differ only in the named moderator, with the other two factors given below the column. A positive value means the component climbs toward rank 1 (more waste relative to the other components).

### Discussion

The overlay supports the first proposition: the components that combine the highest divergence with the lowest capability, positional encoding, layer normalization and the input embedding lookup, form a distinct and stable group. Each is memory-bound and has a positive waste score in every configuration, and for each the published evidence shows a low cost to quality from a different kind of intervention: language models trained without positional encoding reach nearly the same perplexity (Haviv et al., 2022; Kazemnejad et al., 2023), an upper bound for Pythia's partial rotary encoding; normalization can be replaced, though not removed (Zhu et al., 2025; He & Hofmann, 2024); and the embedding can be reduced in dimension at a small cost, though no study removes it (Lan et al., 2020). The capability ranking and the waste ranking are independent: the layer-dropping evidence ranks the feedforward sublayer first for quality (He, Sun, et al., 2024), while the energy divergence sits mainly in the attention core.

The overlay also exposes a tension that the waste score alone does not show. The priority components are the least defensible sources of waste, but they are also small: under the first research question, positional encoding, layer normalization and the lookup together used at most about 7% of the model's energy. The attention core has the largest pooled waste but is too important to remove. The two kinds of divergence identified under the second research question therefore map onto two kinds of target: components whose waste can be removed with little risk but whose total saving is bounded by their small share, and one component whose saving could be large but only through a substitution that computes the same function more efficiently. Which of these yields more energy in practice is a question for the substitutions evaluated under the fifth research question.

The moderator analysis shows that this targeting decision is not sensitive to configuration. Scale reorders the compute-bound components and the loss, as the matrix multiplications take a growing share of larger models (first research question), and precision changed the order in 22 of 72 comparisons, mostly by one place, while raising every memory-bound component's score (second research question); neither moves the priority components by more than one place. Sequence length changes the attention core's score a great deal and its rank hardly at all. The ranking that matters for targeting can therefore be read from any of the profiled configurations, with the exception of the feedforward sublayer at Pythia 6.9B FP32, whose position is not stable across runs.

The overlay has three limits. The capability evidence comes from other models and other kinds of intervention, so it supports an order but not a size: the study cannot say how much quality Pythia would lose without positional encoding, only that the published evidence ranks that loss below the others. The order is applied identically to every configuration, although a component's contribution could itself change with scale or sequence length; for positional encoding in particular, the differences between encodings appear at longer sequence lengths (Kazemnejad et al., 2023). And the quadrant boundary is a convention: the input embedding lookup sits on the median rank, and its membership in the priority group depends on placing that rank in the lower half.
