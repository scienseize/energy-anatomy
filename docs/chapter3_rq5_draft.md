## Draft text

### Research Question 5: Relative to baseline configurations, what energy reduction does each candidate substitution yield?

Each substitution was evaluated in the same session as its baseline, on the same matched passes. Its effect is the change in the model's total energy when the substitute's measured energy replaces that of the component it replaces, as a percentage of the baseline total; a positive value is a saving. Because the substituted total is composed from isolated measurements rather than measured as a whole model, the reduction is an estimate that assumes the unattributed energy is unchanged. The removal of positional encoding runs nothing in its place, so its effect is computed from the measured energy of positional encoding alone.

The published results in Table 10 are not directly comparable with the measured changes in energy, or with each other: each is the source's own headline result, a speed, memory, parameter or quality figure measured on other hardware and models, and none of them is energy. They indicate the benefit each technique claims, not a prediction of its energy saving on the T4. Table 10 gives the measured change for each substitution across the eighteen configurations, and Figure 9 shows it for every configuration.

| No positional encoding, computed (positional encoding) | +0.40% to +4.25% | 18 / 0 | None (removed) | Quality cost: perplexity 20.42 to 20.97 (247M) and 13.05 to 13.10 (1.3B) (Haviv et al., 2022) |

*Note.* Change in model energy is the median over ten matched passes; the range is over the eighteen configurations, and a positive value is a saving. A configuration counts as saving or costing more when the median change is positive or negative and larger than its interquartile range across passes. Measured energy relative to the replaced component is the substitute's energy divided by that of the component it replaces, median over passes, range over configurations. The output-side factorization is this study's extension; the published figure covers the input side only.

**Figure 9.** Change in model energy per substitution, for each of the eighteen configurations (repo `data/figures/F10`). *Note.* Bars show the median over ten passes, and whiskers extend half the interquartile range on each side of the median. Blue bars are savings and red bars are increases.

**Priority components.** The fused rotary kernel saved energy in every configuration, from 0.29% to 3.10% of the model's total, and used 0.22 to 0.44 times the energy of the eager rotary encoding it replaced. Removing positional encoding altogether would save 0.40% to 4.25%, its whole energy share, so the fused kernel recovers 56% to 78% of the removal's saving while computing the same function and carrying no quality cost. For layer normalization, RMSNorm written as separate operators cost more energy than LayerNorm in every configuration, by 0.48% to 4.50% of the model's total, using 1.72 to 3.51 times its energy. The fused RMSNorm changed the model's energy by -0.54% to +0.48%: it saved in 14 configurations and cost more in 3, all at Pythia 1.4B in FP16. The algorithmic change from LayerNorm to RMSNorm is therefore worth close to nothing, while implementing RMSNorm as separate operators costs up to 4.5%; for normalization, the implementation matters more for energy than the algorithm. The factorized embedding saved 1.93% to 21.27%, but almost none of that saving comes from the priority component: replacing the input embedding lookup alone changed the model's energy by -0.01% to +0.11%, less than 1% of the total saving in every configuration. The rest comes from the output side, the factorized projection onto the vocabulary, which is this study's extension and whose quality cost has not been established; shrinking the output embedding of a multilingual encoder to the same 128 dimensions cost 3.6 points (H. W. Chung et al., 2021).

**Comparisons.** The memory-efficient attention kernel saved energy in every configuration, from 0.79% at Pythia 6.9B, 512 tokens, FP32 to 41.54% at Pythia 160M, 2,048 tokens, FP16, recovering 30% to 76% of the attention core's energy while computing the same attention. Its savings follow the attention core's waste score (Spearman correlation 0.96 across the eighteen configurations): they are largest at small models, long sequences and FP16. Linear attention saved up to 45.24% but cost more in two configurations (Pythia 1.4B and 6.9B, both at 512 tokens in FP16) and changes the function, so its quality cost would need separate evidence. Grouped-query attention saved 2.12% to 12.17% in every configuration, recovering 26% to 46% of the attention projections' energy, although the projections carry no positive waste; its saving comes from reduced arithmetic in a compute-bound component, and it too changes the model.

**Predictions against measurements.** The analytical model's predictions held. Across all 126 substitution and configuration pairs, the predicted ratio of the substitute's time to the original's, which takes the larger of its compute time and its memory time, ranked the measured energy ratios with a Spearman correlation of 0.92; predicted memory traffic alone, as given in Table 9, reached 0.68. For memory-bound components the traffic prediction was close: the fused rotary kernel was predicted to move 0.29 times the bytes and used 0.27 times the energy (medians), and the fused RMSNorm was predicted to move the same bytes as LayerNorm and used 0.93 times its energy. RMSNorm as separate operators was predicted to move 6.0 times the bytes and used 3.0 times the energy: the direction held and the size was half. For compute-bound substitutes, energy followed arithmetic rather than traffic: grouped-query attention, compute-bound in every configuration, moved more bytes than the original but performed 0.63 times the operations, and used 0.64 times the energy. The factorized embedding, compute-bound in FP32 and memory-bound in FP16, was predicted to take 0.09 times the time and used 0.08 times the energy. The memory-efficient attention kernel saved less than predicted, using 0.49 times the attention core's energy against a predicted 0.20 times its time.

**Repeatability.** The direction of the main effects repeated on the other two GPUs. In runs 1 and 2, the memory-efficient attention kernel, grouped-query attention, the factorized embedding and the removal of positional encoding saved energy in all eighteen configurations, RMSNorm as separate operators cost more in all eighteen, and linear attention cost more in the same two configurations. The fused RMSNorm, measured in runs 2 and 3, saved in 16 configurations in run 2 and cost more in none. The fused rotary kernel was added for run 3 and has no repeat.

**Published results.** Set beside the published evidence, the measured effects agree in direction where the published result concerns the same mechanism. The fused rotary and RMSNorm kernels were reported as several times faster and lighter in memory than their eager versions, and on the T4 the fused rotary kernel used about a quarter of the eager version's energy. Memory-efficient attention was reported to cut memory sharply at little cost in runtime, and here it saved energy in every configuration. The grouped-query attention result is of a different kind: the published speed-up of more than five times is inference time on a T5-XXL checkpoint converted from multi-head attention with additional training, not a pretraining result, and the pretraining energy saving measured here, 2% to 12%, is far smaller. The published ALBERT result concerns parameters and quality on the input side only, while the measured saving comes almost entirely from the output side.

### Discussion

The results support the second proposition for the substitutions that address a component's cost mechanism directly. Both function-preserving substitutions, the fused rotary kernel and the memory-efficient attention kernel, saved energy in every configuration and in every run in which they were measured, at no cost to what the model computes. The largest savings in the study came from the attention core, the component the capability overlay placed in the high-waste, high-capability quadrant: linear attention saved up to 45.2% of the model's energy but changes the function, while the memory-efficient kernel, which computes the same attention, saved up to 41.5%. The priority components yielded smaller savings, up to 3.1% for the fused rotary kernel and up to 4.25% for removing positional encoding altogether, which is consistent with their small energy shares under the first research question. The tension identified under the third research question therefore resolves in favour of the attention core in absolute terms, provided its function is preserved, while the priority components offer reliable but bounded savings.

The clearest pattern is that implementation matters more than algorithm for the memory-bound components. For positional encoding, the same rotation computed in two fused kernels instead of six eager operators used about a quarter of the energy. For layer normalization, changing the algorithm from LayerNorm to RMSNorm changed little, while implementing RMSNorm as separate operators cost up to 4.5% of the model's energy. For the attention core, the memory-efficient kernel computes exactly the same attention and saved the most. This agrees with the IO-aware view of attention (Dao et al., 2022) and with the kernel-level results for normalization and rotary encoding (Hsu et al., 2024), and it extends them from speed and memory to energy during the forward and backward passes of pretraining.

The analytical model proved useful beyond classification. The roofline-predicted time ranked the measured energy effects of all substitutions with a correlation of 0.92, using only the architecture and the GPU's published limits, which suggests it can screen candidate substitutions before any measurement. It was least accurate for the memory-efficient attention kernel, which saved substantially less than predicted, so a screened candidate still needs to be measured.

Four limits apply. The savings are estimates built from isolated measurements, assuming the rest of the model is unchanged. They were measured on one power-capped GPU type, where energy closely tracks time. The quality cost of the substitutions that change the function, the removal of positional encoding, the factorized embedding, linear attention and grouped-query attention, was not measured; for the output-side factorization, which produces almost all of the factorized embedding's saving, no published estimate exists. And each substitution was evaluated alone, so the savings should not be added together.
